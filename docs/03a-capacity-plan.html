1.1You are right that these are not all the laws

What is on disk is the statutory backbone of five jurisdictions. What is missing is larger than what is present, and the largest missing piece is the one that matters most commercially.

MissingEst. textMultiple of currentConsequence for sizing

Note the ratio: judgments are roughly twelve times the raw text of every statute in the country, and because they are chunked at paragraph level they generate about thirty times as many vectors. Any hosting decision that ignores them is a decision about the demo, not about the product.

1.2Two measured facts that shape the design

  • Legal text compresses 4.17× under gzip — measured on 21.5 MB of core statutes. It is highly repetitive prose, which is good news for the offline pack and for object storage, and only partly good news for Postgres, where compression applies to values above the TOAST threshold and most provisions sit just under it.
  • The average top-level section is ~1.9 KB of text, about 480 tokens. That is almost exactly one embedding chunk, which is why chunking at section level rather than at fixed token windows is both cheaper and more faithful to the citation model.

Two modelling choices that save about 350 MB

Chunks store offsets, not text. A chunk row carries (provision_version_id, char_start, char_end) and derives its text on read. Storing chunk text separately would duplicate the entire corpus — about 158 MB at full scale — for no benefit, since the provision text is already indexed and served.

The tsvector column is not materialised. Indexing the expression rather than storing a generated column halves the full-text cost. It is slightly slower to build and identical to query.

Append-only revisions need a hot-retention boundary. Migration 0012 correctly made re-extraction and re-segmentation retire-and-append so regressions remain diagnosable. Once those revisions had immutable archive manifests and a restore-tested snapshot, superseded derived trees were safely compacted. After migration 0040 materialised compilation expressions, another isolated restore test exposed and fixed ordinal-matching and manifest-reference defects before exactly 20 additional retired trees were archived and pruned. Source PDFs, observations, documents, blocks, OCR evidence, expression manifests and active trees were not deleted. The live database now has 4,797 instrument rows: 4,788 active and nine retired identity-resolution endpoints that must remain for exact proof.

Practically: the live build database is 1,682 MB after post-prune vacuum/analyze. Its 204,509,189-byte dump was restored and signature-checked after parser /24, migrations through 0041, multi-expression materialisation and archive-first compaction. A later serving export may be smaller, but must retain source evidence and use only fail-closed release views.

The compacted forensic build is below 2 GB before retrieval indexes, operational headroom and future revisions. That is not enough margin for a production 2 GB tier; size lexical/vector indexes against the release subset before choosing hosting. The Supabase 500 MB free tier remains suitable only for a deliberately scoped subset, never this complete build database.

Three free-tier facts that bite before storage does

  • The PDFs do not fit anywhere free except R2. 1,857 MB of source documents against a 1 GB Supabase Storage allowance. Cloudflare R2 gives 10 GB with zero egress fees, so blobs go to R2 regardless of which database you choose. That decision is independent and already made.
  • Idle pausing breaks demos. A Supabase free project sleeps after roughly a week of inactivity, which is exactly the cadence of an academic supervisor checking in. Budget for a keep-alive ping or accept a cold first request.
  • No managed host permits ParadeDB. Every managed row above loses real BM25. That is why ILexicalSearch exists in document 01 — the fallback is a tsvector implementation whose ranking delta gets measured on the golden set rather than assumed away.
§4The
unlock

Binary vectors cost us almost nothing

Vectors are the component that decides whether a corpus fits. At full precision they are the largest single line in the model; quantised to bits they are the smallest. The reason we can take that trade without paying for it is already in the architecture.

The measurement that must accompany this. Binary quantisation is a recall trade, and recall is measurable. Before it ships, run the golden set at full precision, at halfvec(384) and at bit(1024), and report Recall@50 and end-to-end NDCG@10 for each. If binary costs more than two points of NDCG after reranking, fall back to halfvec(384) and accept the smaller corpus scope. That ablation belongs in the thesis regardless of the outcome.

§5Hosting
ladder

Four stages, one schema

You asked whether to build locally or on a free tier. The answer is both, in that order — and the reason it is safe to plan it that way is that corpus scope is a deployment parameter, not a schema decision. The same migrations run at every rung.

5.1Stage-by-stage decisions

The recommendation, and the honest counterweight

Own node, running the self-hosted Supabase stack on top of it if you want that developer surface. A custom Postgres image gives you Studio, GoTrue and PostgREST and pg_search — the ergonomics without the lock-out, on the same €65 box.

Supabase Pro is still the right answer if nobody on the team is willing to be paged, if the FYP timeline cannot absorb learning pgBackRest and monitoring, or if you need a compliance posture you can point at rather than one you operate. Those are real reasons, and $300 a month is a defensible price for not being on call. They are just not architectural reasons — and the architecture should not be shaped around them, which is why ILexicalSearch exists.

5.3Building the whole thing locally first

Yes, and it is the right sequence. A full-scale production database — every statute, every judgment, every embedding, every index — fits on a laptop, and the build is measured in hours rather than days.

pgAdmin4 is a fine viewer and a terrible source of truth

Use pgAdmin4, DBeaver or DataGrip to browse, query and inspect — all good. But if the schema exists only because someone clicked it into being in a local session, it cannot be reproduced on the server, and the move to production becomes a manual re-do with silent drift. Two rules make the local build genuinely portable:

  • The schema is migrations in git. Alembic, sqitch, or numbered SQL files — the tool matters far less than the rule. Every index, extension, constraint and role is created by a migration that runs identically anywhere.
  • The engine is a pinned Docker image. The same image runs on the laptop and on the server, so extension versions cannot diverge. Build it once from postgres:17 plus pgvector, pg_search and ltree, and pin the digest.

Parity checklist before you move anywhere

  • Postgres major version pinned and identical — a restore across major versions needs a dump, not a physical copy.
  • Extension versions pinned in the image and asserted by a migration that fails loudly on mismatch.
  • Locale and collation identical. A different lc_collate silently changes index ordering and text comparison, and it is a genuinely nasty class of bug.
  • The golden-set evaluation runs green on the target before traffic moves. If NDCG@10 shifts, the lexical implementation changed and you now know by how much.
  • A restore drill completed on the target from the R2 repository, with the evaluation suite run against the restored copy.

Two things worth noticing. First, the semantic tier is mostly the encoder, not the vectors — 25 MB of model against 7 MB of index — which is exactly why it is an optional tier rather than a default, and why the answer tier that depends on it is offered alongside it. Second, the answer tier is the cheapest capability in the whole product: 12 MB of download buys grounded, cited, pre-verified answers to the head of the query distribution, working with no network and no model on the device.

The offline quality ladder, stated honestly

  • Statutory lookup and browse — identical to online. The pack is signed and authoritative.
  • Lexical search — identical to online where the online host is also on tsvector; slightly weaker than a pg_search baseline.
  • Semantic search — measurably weaker: 384-dim on device against 1024-dim on the server, with no cross-encoder. Labelled “offline search” in the UI.
  • Grounded answers — identical to online for questions the answer tier covers, unavailable otherwise. Never approximated.
  • Precedent, drafting, novel questions — unavailable, and the UI says so rather than degrading quietly.

7.1Answering the question directly

How much space do the laws take?243 MB of raw text for the 4,710 documents on disk; 58 MB compressed; 1,857 MB as source PDFs. In a searchable Postgres with vectors and full-text indexes, 744 MB with binary quantisation.
Can Supabase free hold it?Not all of it. It holds federal statutes comfortably — 248 MB against a 500 MB ceiling — with RAG fully built: vectors, full-text, provision tree, cross-references, users and audit. It cannot hold all five jurisdictions, and it is nowhere near case law.
Is there a better free option?For scope, Oracle Cloud Always Free — ~200 GB and your own Postgres with pg_search, at the cost of operating it and accepting capacity risk. For convenience, Supabase. Aiven’s 1 GB fits all statutes if you want managed and complete.
Should we build locally first?Yes. Ingest and gate the full corpus on a local Postgres with every extension available, then publish a scoped subset upward. Developing against a 500 MB ceiling would distort exactly the decisions that are hardest to reverse.
Does the phone stay reasonable?135 MB installed for app, core statutes, semantic search and pre-verified offline answers. The full statutory corpus is a 400 MB opt-in that most users will never need.
